Designing Human-Centered Interfaces for Adaptive Learning Systems

Alex Morgan, Priya Shah, and Daniel Kim
Department of Computer Science, Northbridge University, New York, USA

alex.morgan@northbridge.edu Paper ID: 014 Track: Human–Computer Interaction

Abstract— Adaptive learning systems increasingly rely on interface decisions to translate complex learner data into clear, actionable feedback. This paper presents a lightweight interaction framework that combines progressive disclosure, contextual prompts, and transparent recommendation explanations. A mixed-method evaluation with 42 participants indicates that the proposed framework improves task confidence while preserving completion speed.

Index Terms— adaptive systems, human–computer interaction, learning analytics, interface design, explainable recommendations.

I. Introduction

Digital learning environments collect a growing volume of information about learner behavior. Yet the presence of data does not guarantee that learners can understand or act upon it. Poorly structured feedback can increase cognitive load and reduce trust in an otherwise useful system.

We investigate how interface structure can make adaptive recommendations more legible. Our work focuses on three research questions: how feedback should be layered, how system reasoning should be exposed, and which interaction patterns support confident action.

II. Related Work

Research in intelligent tutoring systems has explored personalized sequencing, mastery estimation, and automated hints. Recent work has shifted attention toward learner agency and the interpretability of algorithmic decisions.

Our approach builds on these findings by treating the interface as an active part of the recommendation system rather than a passive presentation layer. This position is consistent with prior studies on explanation quality and user control.

III. Proposed Framework

The framework organizes feedback into three layers. The first layer presents an immediate next action. The second gives a short explanation of the recommendation. The third exposes optional evidence for learners who want to inspect the underlying signal.

C = αA + βE + γU (1)

Here, C represents perceived clarity, while A, E, and U represent actionability, explanation quality, and user control, respectively. Weights were calibrated during a pilot study.

IV. Methodology

We conducted a controlled usability study with 42 participants divided into a baseline group and an adaptive-interface group. Participants completed a sequence of information-seeking tasks and rated their confidence after each task.

  1. Review a recommended learning objective.
  2. Inspect the supporting explanation.
  3. Select and complete the next activity.

Completion time, error rate, and perceived confidence were recorded. Semi-structured interviews were then used to identify recurring expectations and points of friction.

V. Results

Participants using the proposed interface completed tasks with a comparable median time and reported higher confidence than the baseline group. The largest improvement occurred in tasks where a recommendation required additional context.

The results suggest that explanation controls are most effective when they remain close to the recommended action. Moving explanations into a separate help area increased navigation effort and reduced engagement.

VI. Discussion

These findings reinforce the importance of designing explanations as part of the primary interaction. The framework does not attempt to expose every model detail; instead, it prioritizes the smallest amount of information needed to support a meaningful decision.

VII. Conclusion

This paper introduced a human-centered framework for adaptive learning interfaces. Future work will evaluate the framework over longer periods and examine how explanation preferences change as learner familiarity increases.
